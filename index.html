How Earth to MIDI works

  

    
What it is

    
Earth and space data for live events. The page reads public live feeds and turns each reading into MIDI. Discrete events, like a quake or an asteroid pass, become notes. Continuous values, like wind speed or the Kp index, become controllers (CC, 0 to 127).


    
What is loopMIDI, and why do you need it?

    
loopMIDI is a free Windows program by Tobias Erichsen that creates virtual MIDI ports. Think of each port as a software cable: one program sends MIDI into it, and another program reads MIDI out of it.

    
You need it because a web page can only send MIDI to ports your computer already knows about, and Windows has no built-in way to pass MIDI between programs. With a loopMIDI port, this page sends the data, and your DAW, Chataigne or lighting software receives it as if it came from a hardware controller.

    
On macOS you don’t need it, because the built-in IAC Driver (in Audio MIDI Setup) does the same job. loopMIDI is free for personal use, so check the terms on its website if you plan to use it commercially.

Globe view

    
Switch the map to a 3D globe with the Globe button on the map (or the G key, or View in the Layers menu). Everything on the flat map appears on the globe: the layers, the twilight and aurora, the pulses and markers, the ships, aircraft and lanes. Click any point for its details, just as on the flat map.

    
    
      
  • Spin: the globe turns slowly by itself in Earth’s own direction. Use the Spin slider in the Layers menu to change the speed, from stopped up to about 30 degrees a second (finer control at the slow end). Real time sets Earth’s true rotation, one turn in 23 hours 56 minutes. Reverse turns it the other way.
    • 
      
  • Turn it yourself: drag the globe to rotate it and tilt it (on a touch screen, drag sideways). Double-click to level the tilt. The globe pauses its spin while you hold it.
    • 
      
  • Notes: the globe needs WebGL, which almost every current browser has; if yours does not, it says so and stays on the flat map. Labels bend near the edge of the globe, and details near the poles are stretched, as on any flat map wrapped around a sphere. Your choice of view and speed is remembered, and the globe works in kiosk mode too.

Kiosk mode

    
A clean full-screen display for installations and public screens. Press the Kiosk button at the top, or the K key. The page fills the screen with just the map, the title and clock, and a large live feed of what is happening. The controls hide, the screen is kept awake where the browser allows it, and the cursor disappears when idle. You can still tap a dot for its details. Press Esc or K again, or move the mouse and use Exit kiosk.

    
If the Step sequencer is on, the kiosk also shows the current sequence along the bottom: every source as a row, time across the top, the lit steps in each source’s colour, and a white frame around the step playing now. A line above it reads out the step, when the next step starts, and which sources are playing right now. When the sequencer is off, the map simply uses the whole screen.

    
To start straight in kiosk mode, open the page with #kiosk on the end of its address (for example earth-midi.html#kiosk). Browsers only allow true full screen after a touch or click, so it fills the window at once and goes fully full screen on the first tap. For a screen that never shows browser bars, launch Chrome or Edge with its --kiosk option as well.


    
Themes

    
The Theme panel in the side column changes the look of the whole page, including the map and the kiosk display. Choose Deep sea (the default dark blue), Forest green or Earth, a natural brown. Custom colours lets you pick four colours yourself: the background, the panels, the text and the accent (used for buttons, links and highlights). The page works out the borders, softer text and map colour from those. If the text and panel colours are too close, a warning says so. Your choice is remembered, and Reset puts the custom colours back to their starting values. The data colours, such as the ones for quakes and the ISS, stay the same in every theme so they remain easy to tell apart.


    
Pause and resume

    
Pause all (top right, or the Space bar) silences everything at once. It sends all-notes-off, stops the loops and sequencers, and holds the controllers where they are. Data keeps updating in the background, and quakes that arrive while paused are skipped. Resume sends the current controller values again and restarts the loops from the beginning. A “Paused” label shows on the map, including in full screen.

Controller mapping

    
Every data stream can be sent as CC, pitch bend or aftertouch, on its own channel and CC number. Range % zooms in on the part of the data you care about and stretches it to 0 to 127. Invert flips it and Smooth slows the changes. A live bar shows each stream’s output. CV out also sends the stream as a control voltage (see CV output). Your setup saves automatically. Use Export and Import to move it between machines, and Reset to go back to the defaults.

MIDI monitor

    
The monitor in the side panel shows the latest messages as they are sent, with what triggered each one: for example a wildfire, a quake and its location, a city’s weather, an asteroid or a flare class. Controller lines show the module and stream. It also works in preview mode with no port selected.

    
Use the filters above the monitor to focus on what matters: switch message types on or off (notes, controllers, pitch bend, aftertouch), tick only the sources you want, pick a channel, or search for a word such as “wildfire”. Pause freezes the display so you can read it, Clear empties it and Show all resets the filters. The filters only change what you see here and in the full-screen display, not what is sent. Your choices are remembered.

    
Wider docks the monitor across the full width of the screen, handy for long labels. In that mode Taller makes the panel bigger, and Narrower puts it back in the side panel. Your size is remembered too.


    
The map

    
    
      
  • Click any dot, a note pulse, a lightning strike, a ship, a strait, the ISS, the sun or the moon for details. Pulses show what triggered them and have a button to play that note again. Quakes, events and cities have a button to play just that item.
    • 
      
  • ISS ground track: the dashed line shows where the station will be over roughly the next 2 hours, with markers every 30 minutes. It is a simple prediction, so it drifts over time.
    • 
      
  • Sun and moon: each marker sits at the point on Earth directly beneath it, and the moon shows its current phase. The shading shows night, with three lighter bands for twilight: civil, nautical and astronomical, where the sun is 6, 12 and 18 degrees below the horizon.
    • 
      
  • Aurora oval: the green to pink glow around each pole is NOAA’s live aurora forecast: the brighter and pinker, the higher the chance of visible aurora. The oval grows toward the equator in magnetic storms. Click any glowing area for the probability there. It refreshes every 5 minutes and needs an internet connection.
    • 
      
  • Tectonic plate boundaries: the orange lines mark where the Earth’s plates meet. Quakes and volcanoes cluster along them, so watch the quake dots trace them out. The lines come from the PB2002 plate model.
    • 
      
  • Labels: switch on City and strait labels in Layers to name the major cities and, with Shipping lanes on, the straits. Labels that would overlap are skipped, so full screen shows the most.
    • 
      
  • Layers opens a drawer that slides out from the right and scrolls, so every option is reachable on any screen. Close it with its Close button, the Esc key or by clicking elsewhere. It shows or hides each part of the map, in five groups. View: flat map or globe, with spin speed. Map: coastlines, grid, day, night and twilight, tectonic plate boundaries, city and strait labels (coastlines and plate boundaries need an internet connection). Sky: sun, moon, aurora oval, ISS and its ground track. Earth data: quake history, natural events, weather cities, lightning, sea gradient, ocean current flow. Transport: shipping lanes and straits, ships sailing the lanes, live ships, aircraft. Live activity: note pulses and markers, births and deaths, sunrise and sunset.
    • 
      
  • Pulses and markers: every note that plays on the map draws a ring and leaves a dot that fades over 8 seconds, so you can click it. Switch births and deaths or sunrise and sunset off separately if the map gets busy.
    • 
      
  • Full screen (button or F) gives a clean display with a live monitor and log. The cursor and buttons hide after a few seconds without movement.

Transport

    
Ships (live, Baltic) shows real vessels broadcasting AIS, the tracking signal ships carry, from the free Digitraffic service run by the Finnish transport agency. Its coverage is the Baltic Sea and Finnish waters, typically hundreds to a couple of thousand ships. Worldwide live ship tracking is only available from paid or keyed services, so this is the open source that works with no sign-up. Each ship appears on the map as a dot with a heading line (dimmer if it is stationary), and the ships are played as a looping west to east sweep: latitude sets pitch, speed sets velocity and length. Click a ship for its identifier, speed, heading and status.

    
Aircraft shows live flights from the OpenSky Network, a community network of ADS-B receivers that picks up the position, height, speed and heading each aircraft broadcasts. Choose the whole world, Europe, North America or Asia and the Pacific. Planes appear as small dots, brighter the higher they fly, and drift along their heading between updates so the map keeps moving. Click one for its callsign, country, height, speed and heading. The airspace is played as a looping west to east sweep, with height setting pitch and speed setting loudness and length, and the controllers follow the numbers in the air, average height and speed, and how many are climbing or descending.

    
OpenSky’s free anonymous access allows about 400 credits a day and each update costs 4, so the page updates every 15 to 60 minutes (30 by default) and reuses its last update when you reload, to save credits. If the limit is reached the card says so and tries again later. OpenSky data is for research and non-commercial use.

    
If OpenSky cannot be reached from your browser (some networks and browsers block it), the page switches by itself to community ADS-B feeds: adsb.lol, then adsb.one, then airplanes.live. They only answer for a circle around a point, so the page asks about 6 to 32 busy airport areas, depending on the Area you chose, and shows the aircraft it finds there. That is a rich sample of the busiest airspace, not every plane, and it refreshes every 4 minutes. The card says which feed is in use. It tries OpenSky again from time to time.

    
Ships sailing the lanes are a modelled fleet of about 700 vessels that sail slowly along the dashed lanes, so the world’s sea routes are alive on the map and the globe. They are not real ships: they follow typical routes at typical speeds (about 20 to 40 km/h), and the Ship sailing speed menu on the Shipping lanes card sets how much faster than real life they are shown (3000 times faster by default, which makes an Atlantic crossing take a few minutes; choose 10000 times for a livelier sea, or 300 and Real time for a calmer one). Click one to see its route and speed. Turn them off with Ships sailing the lanes in Layers. The real ships on the Ships (live) card stay at their true speed, which is too slow to see on a world map, and the open feed only covers the Baltic.

    
Shipping lanes (modelled) is a model, not a live feed. The dashed lines are approximate major routes, and the diamonds are 12 busy straits and canals, such as Malacca, Suez, Panama, Hormuz and the English Channel. Random ships pass each strait at its typical daily rate (rounded, and real figures vary from year to year), sped up by the Speed setting. Each transit pulses on the map and can play a note; bigger ships play longer notes. Use the Layers menu to hide the lanes or the ships.


    
Aurora oval

    
NOAA’s OVATION model forecasts where aurora is likely for roughly the next 30 minutes, as a probability on a 1 degree grid, refreshed every 5 minutes. On the map it glows around both poles, and a click shows the probability at that spot. The Aurora module plays it as a ring: the strongest points around both poles become notes in a west to east sweep, higher nearer the pole and louder and longer where aurora is more likely. In a geomagnetic storm the oval swells toward the equator and the notes get lower and more numerous. Its controllers give the peak probability, how much of the Earth has aurora, and how far each oval reaches.


    
Ocean currents and the sea gradient

    
The seas are drawn as a colour gradient with thousands of tiny streaks showing which way the water flows. The data comes from the Open-Meteo Marine API: ocean current speed and direction, sea surface temperature and wave height. It is sampled on a grid of 540 points around the world and refreshed every 6 hours; the last update is kept so reloading the page does not use up free requests.

    
    
      
  • Colour the seas by: in the Layers drawer, choose Current speed (the default), Sea temperature or Wave height. The colour bar at the left of the map shows the scale. The gradient is only drawn over water, and it works on the flat map and on the globe.
    • 
      
  • Flow: the white streaks drift along the currents. Each streak points the way the water is going, and is longer where the current is faster. Flow animation sets how fast they drift (the animation is far faster than the real currents, which move only a few kilometres an hour). Switch Sea gradient and Ocean current flow on or off separately in the Layers drawer.
    • 
      
  • Click the sea for the conditions at that spot: current speed and direction, sea temperature and wave height.
    • 
      
  • Sound: the Ocean currents card plays the 150 strongest currents as a west to east sweep: warmer water sounds higher, faster currents louder, bigger waves longer. The controllers give the average current speed, the fastest current, the average sea temperature and wave height, the average heading of the strong currents, and the share of the ocean with strong currents.
    • 
    

    
Ocean data: Open-Meteo Marine API (non-commercial use), with currents and sea temperature from the Copernicus Marine Service (Mercator Ocean and Météo-France SMOC) and waves from MFWAM. The currents are a model at about 8 km resolution, not measurements, and are not suitable for navigation.


    
Planet clock

    
The turning of the Earth as music. A note plays at every sunrise and sunset over 34 major population centres: sunrise in the upper half of the pitch range, sunset in the lower half, bigger cities louder and longer. That is about one event every 20 minutes, and each is labelled in the monitor (for example “sunrise over Lagos”). An optional chime plays on every UTC hour. The controllers give you a smooth clock: UTC time of day and Earth’s rotation angle as repeating ramps, the year as a very slow ramp, and the share of people in daylight and awake (7am to 11pm local solar time).


    
Earth motion and forces

    
Live values calculated from orbital maths: Earth’s speed around the Sun (about 29.3 to 30.3 km/s, fastest in January), its distance from the Sun, the strength of sunlight, how far away the Moon is, and the pull of the Moon on the tides, including the monthly spring and neap cycle. The card also shows the spin speed, the force of the Sun’s and Moon’s gravity in newtons, the next solstice or equinox, and approximate speeds around the galaxy. Calculations are accurate enough for music and display, not for navigation. An optional heartbeat note, shaped by these values, plays every 10, 30 or 60 seconds.


    
Lightning

    
Real lightning strikes from the Blitzortung.org community network, arriving as they happen. It is off by default, because the worldwide feed uses roughly 1 GB of data a day while it is on. Switch it on with the On switch on the Lightning card (Earth tab), and off again to close the feed.

    
Every strike is counted and drawn as a dot on the map that fades over 2 minutes. Because there can be dozens a second, notes are limited by Max notes per second and can be restricted to one part of the world. Controllers follow every strike: strike rate, the latest position, and a pulse (CC119) on each one. The data is for personal, non-commercial use and the feed is unofficial, so it can change or refuse connections from web pages. If the card says it could not connect, that is the likely reason.


    
Births and deaths

    
There is no true live feed of births and deaths, because nobody counts them in real time. The world population counters you see online are models, and so is this. The page reads the latest World Bank figures for world population, birth rate and death rate, turns them into an average number of births and deaths per second, and then generates random events at those rates. Each event is placed near a major population centre, in proportion to population, with younger regions weighted towards births and older regions towards deaths. The locations are modelled, not real.

    
Births and deaths are separate modules on channels 11 and 12, each with its own Note output settings. Play one note per thins the notes so it isn’t too busy, though every event still counts, pulses on the map and fires its pulse controller (CC113 and CC116). The cards show estimated totals since midnight UTC.


    
Step sequencer

    
An optional planner, like the step sequencer on an analogue synth, for installations that run for hours or days. Open the grid in the Step sequencer panel under the map. Time runs across the top, split into steps, and the sources run down the side. A lit step means that source may play during that part of the cycle, and a dark step silences its notes. Activate turns it on; while it is off, everything plays as normal.

    
    
      
  • Cycle: how long one pass takes, from 5 minutes to 24 hours. It then repeats. Steps sets how many columns it is split into (8 to 96), and the panel shows how long each step lasts.
    • 
      
  • Lock to the clock: ties the cycle to your computer’s clock, starting at midnight, so a 24 hour cycle follows the time of day and the times along the top are real clock times. With it off, the cycle starts from when you activate or change it.
    • 
      
  • Editing: click or drag to turn steps on and off. Scroll over a step, or press the up and down arrow keys, to transpose it up or down in semitones (shift for octaves); the amount shows in the step. Double-click a step to reset its pitch. On a phone or tablet, use the Tap does menu to switch between turning steps on and off and changing their pitch. Click a source name to switch its whole row, or a time label to switch a whole column.
    • 
      
  • Fill: quick patterns: all on, clear, random, each source taking a turn, Earth then Space then Transport, or a build-up where sources join one by one.
    • 
      
  • Also gate controllers: by default only notes (and CV gates) follow the sequencer, and controllers keep running. Tick this to hold controllers while a source is switched off.
    • 
    

    
The sequencer gates and transposes notes at the moment they sound, including the loops, so a looping source follows the pattern as the cycle moves. It does not change the data or the sources’ own settings. Your pattern and settings are saved in the browser, and stay active after a reload, which suits an unattended installation. They are also included when you Export and Import the mapping. For example, a 24 hour cycle with Earth, then Space, then Transport gives a day that moves through three moods.


    
Built-in sound

    
To hear and see it working without any MIDI, switch on Built-in sound (in the MIDI output panel, the Sound button at the top, or press M). Every note that plays also sounds in your browser. Choose from three sounds:

    
    
      
  • Chime: a bright, short bell.
    • 
      
  • Calm: a soft, glassy tone with a gentle start and a long release of several seconds, for quiet spaces.
    • 
      
  • Pad: a warm, slowly swelling pad built from detuned layers, with a filter that opens as each note blooms and a very long fade.
    • 
    

    
Reverb adds a large, soft space and is strongest on Calm and Pad. Turn it down for a drier sound. Notes are shifted by whole octaves into a comfortable range for each sound, so the pitch names match but very low notes sound higher. Overlapping notes are limited to protect your computer. Controllers are silent. Browsers only allow audio after a click, so it always starts off, and Pause all silences it too.

    
For an installation, try Calm or Pad at a modest volume with the births, deaths, planet clock and weather modules: together they make a slow, shifting ambient field.


    
CV output (modular)

    
If you have a MIDI-to-CV box, you don’t need this. The normal MIDI output (notes, controllers, pitch bend) already drives it. This option is for sending real control voltage straight from the computer, using a DC-coupled multichannel audio interface such as the Expert Sleepers ES-9 or ES-8. An ordinary audio interface blocks DC and will not work.

    
Tick Also send control voltage (CV) in the CV output panel, choose your interface, and set its full scale (what 1.0 equals; check your interface’s manual). Then give each module outputs under Note output: pitch (1 volt per octave, with the 0 V note you choose), gate (5 V while a note plays, with a brief dip between notes so envelopes retrigger) and velocity (0 to 5 V). Each stream in the Controller mapping also has a CV out menu that sends it as 0 to 5 V. Outputs are numbered from 1; only as many as your interface has will work. CV is monophonic per output, so the latest note wins.

    
Use Test: sweep output 1 to check the connection with a meter or scope. Pause all and Panic drop the gates to 0 V. Safety: never connect CV outputs to speakers, headphones or line inputs. DC can damage them. Use Chrome or Edge, which can pick the output device.


    
Keys

    
Space pause or resume everything, M built-in sound on or off, G globe or flat map, K kiosk mode, F full screen, ? this help, Esc closes popups and full screen.


    
Data sources

    
USGS and EMSC earthquakes, wheretheiss.at, NOAA space weather (solar wind, GOES X-ray and proton flux, storm scales, aurora forecast), NASA EONET, Open-Meteo, NASA NeoWs and DONKI, the World Bank (population, birth and death rates), Open-Meteo Marine (ocean currents, sea temperature, waves), Fintraffic Digitraffic (ships), the OpenSky Network, adsb.lol, adsb.one and airplanes.live (aircraft), Blitzortung.org (lightning) Natural Earth coastlines and the PB2002 tectonic plate boundaries (via GitHub). The planet clock and Earth motion modules are calculated on your computer from astronomical formulas and need no internet. An internet connection is needed. The only things stored are your settings and your NASA key, in this browser.

    
NASA API key: the asteroid and solar flare feeds use a shared demo key by default, which is rate-limited and refreshes every 3 hours. For faster updates (every 30 minutes), get a free key at api.nasa.gov, paste it into the NASA API key box on the Asteroids or Solar flares card (Space tab) and press Enter. Both cards share the one key.


    
If something is not working

    
    
      
  • No sound: check loopMIDI is running with a port created, the port is selected here, enabled as an input in your software, and that a track is listening on that channel.
    • 
      
  • Built-in sound is silent: switch Sound on first (browsers need a click before playing audio), raise the volume, and check your device volume and that the module’s notes are on.
    • 
      
  • “Web MIDI not supported”: use Chrome or Edge on a desktop. If MIDI is blocked when opening the file directly, serve it from localhost.
    • 
      
  • A card shows an error: that feed is unreachable or rate-limited. The others keep working. For NASA rate limits, add your own key. “HTTP 503” means NASA’s servers are briefly down: the page keeps showing the last good data and retries every 5 minutes.
    • 
      
  • Ships card says it could not reach Digitraffic, or shows few ships: the open feed covers only the Baltic Sea and Finnish waters, and some browsers or networks block it. The rest of the page keeps working.
    • 
      
  • Aircraft card says the limit is used up, or that OpenSky is not reachable: the page falls back to community feeds by itself and says so. If it reports that it could not reach any aircraft feed, your network or an ad blocker is probably blocking them. Keep the OpenSky interval at 15 minutes or more, and avoid reloading the page repeatedly.
    • 
      
  • No coastlines or plate boundaries: they load from the internet. Check your connection, or ignore them, since the map works without.
    • 
      
  • Too busy: raise the minimum magnitude, lengthen the loops, or narrow a module’s “Only play when” filter.
